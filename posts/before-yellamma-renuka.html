THE GODS WHO WERE FORGOTTEN

🔥 Before Yellamma: Who Was Renuka Really? The Story Behind the Goddess

By Sakshi Madgundi · 2026

There is a name that appears in temples, village traditions, Puranic stories and devotional songs across parts of India:

Renuka.

Today, she is worshipped in different forms and under different names — Renuka Devi, Yellamma, Renukamba, and others. In the familiar Sanskritic story, she is remembered primarily as the wife of the sage Jamadagni and the mother of Parashurama, the warrior-sage regarded as an avatar of Vishnu.

But there is a question hiding underneath that familiar story:

Was Renuka always simply “the mother of Parashurama”?

The answer is more complicated than the usual story suggests.

And that is where her story becomes much more interesting.

The Renuka We Usually Hear About

The best-known story places Renuka in the household of the sage Jamadagni.

She is described as exceptionally devoted and powerful through her ascetic discipline. In one famous episode, her spiritual power is associated with her ability to perform an extraordinary daily task: carrying water in an unbaked clay pot.

Then one day, something changes.

Renuka sees a group of celestial beings bathing in the river. Her concentration falters. The unbaked pot can no longer hold together.

When she returns home, Jamadagni becomes furious.

He commands his sons to kill their mother.

Several refuse.

Parashurama obeys.

Jamadagni, pleased with his son's obedience, grants him a boon. Parashurama asks that his mother be brought back to life.

Renuka is restored.

The basic episode appears in Sanskrit literary traditions including the Mahabharata and later Puranic literature, although the details vary between versions.

But something important is easy to miss.

In these early narrative contexts, Renuka is not necessarily the central character.

The story is largely being told as part of the larger story of Parashurama.

Renuka is his mother.

Jamadagni is his father.

The episode explains something about Parashurama's character, obedience and extraordinary place within the tradition.

But later, Renuka begins to become something much larger.

The Woman Behind the Mother

When we move beyond the familiar Parashurama story, Renuka starts appearing in a much wider religious landscape.

She is worshipped in parts of Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Telangana and Tamil Nadu , sometimes under names or forms connected with local mother-goddess traditions. Renuka and Yellamma became closely associated in several South Indian traditions.

And this is where we have to be careful.

It would be tempting to say:

“Renuka was originally an ancient goddess, and later Hindu texts simply turned her into Parashurama's mother.”

But there isn't enough evidence to state that as an established historical fact.

Instead, what we can see is something more fascinating:

multiple traditions appear to have converged around the figure of Renuka.

One academic study describes the development of Renuka's mother-goddess identity as a complex process involving the merging of different personalities and myths.

In other words, the Renuka worshipped in a village shrine does not necessarily have to be understood through only one Sanskrit story.

Her identity grew through text, oral tradition, regional worship and local goddess traditions.

From Renuka to Yellamma

One of the most important transformations happens in Karnataka and neighbouring regions.

Renuka becomes associated with Yellamma.

Yellamma is not simply a different spelling of Renuka. The traditions surrounding her developed within particular regional and social contexts, and there are multiple versions of her story.

Researchers studying Yellamma specifically note that versions of her legend differ from region to region.

That means there isn't one universally fixed “Yellamma story.”

Instead, there are many Renukas.

There is the Renuka of Sanskrit epic and Puranic literature.

There is the Renuka of regional temple traditions.

There is Yellamma of northern Karnataka.

There are traditions identifying her with other local mother goddesses.

And there is the Renuka worshipped at important shrines such as Mahur in Maharashtra.

The boundaries between these traditions are not always neat.

And perhaps that is the point.

Was Renuka Originally a Goddess?

This is where things become controversial.

Some modern writers and researchers have proposed that the Renuka/Yellamma tradition absorbed or was connected with older local mother-goddess traditions.

One study argues that the later Renuka-Yellamma tradition may represent a process in which local goddess traditions became connected with the Sanskritic story of Renuka and Parashurama.

But claims such as “Renuka was definitely a pre-Vedic goddess” or “Renuka was definitely a historical queen” go beyond what the evidence can securely establish.

There are later regional accounts that portray Renuka in very different ways — including traditions that give her royal or local origins. One modern study examining these traditions, for example, discusses accounts connecting her with the Vidarbha region and with a possible queenly identity. These are regional/traditional interpretations, not established historical biography .

And that distinction matters.

Because the mystery isn't solved by replacing one legend with another.

Then What Happened to Her?

Perhaps the most remarkable part of Renuka's story is what happens after her death and resurrection.

In later traditions, the episode becomes connected with ideas about Renuka's transformation into a goddess.

Some versions contain an extraordinary detail: because Parashurama was in haste while restoring his mother, the head and body were mistakenly joined incorrectly.

This becomes one explanation for unusual forms of Renuka/Yellamma worship in some regional traditions.

The story therefore stops being merely:

“Parashurama's mother was killed and brought back.”

It becomes:

“A woman who crossed death became a goddess whose identity could transcend the boundaries of caste, family and ordinary human life.”

Different communities interpreted this transformation differently.

And over time, Renuka's name became attached to traditions that were much broader than the original Parashurama narrative.

The Goddess With Many Names

Across different regions, Renuka/Yellamma is associated with several names and forms.

She may appear as:

  • Renuka
  • Yellamma
  • Renukamba
  • Ekvira
  • Yamai
  • and other regional forms of the mother goddess.

This doesn't necessarily mean that all these goddesses began as exactly the same deity.

Rather, it shows how Indian religious traditions have historically allowed local and wider traditions to overlap.

A village goddess could become identified with a figure known from Sanskrit literature.

A Sanskrit figure could acquire new stories in a regional tradition.

A mother goddess could be given a new genealogy.

And a story could travel from text into song, temple ritual and oral tradition.

Renuka is a remarkable example of that process.

So… Who Was Renuka Before Yellamma?

The honest answer is:

We don't have enough evidence to give her one definitive “original identity.”

What we can establish is that the Renuka known from Sanskrit traditions became connected over time with much broader regional goddess traditions.

In the Mahabharata and Puranic narratives, she is prominently remembered as Jamadagni's wife and Parashurama's mother.

In later regional traditions, she becomes a powerful mother goddess in her own right.

And in Yellamma traditions, she takes on an identity that cannot be reduced to simply being Parashurama's mother.

That transformation is perhaps more interesting than a simple origin story.

Because Renuka's story isn't only about who she was.

It is about how a figure changes when a story travels across centuries, regions and communities.

The Renuka We Remember

Today, when someone says “Renuka,” they might be thinking about Parashurama.

Someone else might think of Renuka Devi at Mahur.

Someone in Karnataka might think of Yellamma.

Someone else may know her through a local village goddess tradition.

All of them are looking at the same name through different layers of tradition.

And perhaps this is why Renuka belongs in a series called:

The Gods Who Were Forgotten

Because maybe Renuka was never really forgotten.

Perhaps we simply forgot that she had more than one story.

A Note on Sources

The story of Renuka and Parashurama occurs in multiple Sanskrit traditions, including the Mahabharata and Puranic literature, while the identification of Renuka with Yellamma and other regional goddesses belongs to later and regional religious traditions.

These traditions contain variations, so this article distinguishes between textual narratives, regional traditions and modern scholarly interpretations rather than presenting one version as the single historical truth.

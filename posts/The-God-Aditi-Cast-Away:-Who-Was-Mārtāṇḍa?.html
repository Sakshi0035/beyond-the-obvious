The God Aditi Cast Away: Who Was Mārtāṇḍa?

There is a strange story hidden inside one of the oldest surviving collections of Indian sacred poetry.

It begins with Aditi.

She had eight sons.

Seven of them went with her to the gods.

But the eighth?

She cast him away.

His name was Mārtāṇḍa.

And what makes his story even stranger is what happens next.

Aditi eventually brought him back — for birth and for death.

So who exactly was Mārtāṇḍa?

Was he an ancient form of the Sun?

Was he connected with the origin of mortal beings?

Or was he something even older that later traditions came to understand differently?

The Rigveda never gives us a simple answer.

The Story Begins With Aditi

Today, Aditi is widely remembered as a divine mother and the mother of the Ādityas. But the oldest Vedic material does not always present mythology in the neat genealogies familiar from later Hindu traditions.

One of the most mysterious passages appears in Rigveda 10.72, a hymn concerned with the generations and emergence of the gods.

The hymn says that Aditi had eight sons born from her body.

But she went to the gods with only seven.

The eighth was Mārtāṇḍa.

And he was cast away.

Why?

The hymn doesn't tell us.

There is no dramatic explanation. No battle. No punishment. No story about something Mārtāṇḍa had done wrong.

Just a remarkably strange statement:

Aditi had eight sons — and rejected one.

That one was Mārtāṇḍa.

The Name Mārtāṇḍa Is Strange Too

The mystery becomes even deeper when we look at his name.

The Sanskrit name Mārtāṇḍa has traditionally been connected with the idea of a “dead egg” or something associated with a lifeless egg. Different translations and interpretations of the ancient verse vary.

That creates an extraordinary image.

Aditi gives birth to eight sons.

Seven become associated with the gods.

But the eighth is connected with something that is dead, incomplete or lifeless.

And yet...

He is brought back.

Why Did Aditi Bring Him Back?

This may be the strangest part of the entire story.

The next verse says that Aditi went forward with her seven sons, but brought Mārtāṇḍa back for procreation and death.

Think about what that means.

Mārtāṇḍa isn't simply rejected forever.

He has a purpose.

And that purpose is connected with the two things that define mortal existence:

Birth.

And death.

This is why Mārtāṇḍa has remained such an intriguing figure for interpreters.

Was Mārtāṇḍa the Sun?

Here's where the story becomes complicated.

A common later understanding connects Mārtāṇḍa with the Sun.

Later Vedic and post-Vedic traditions use Mārtāṇḍa as a name or epithet associated with Sūrya, and traditional commentators also identify him with the Sun.

There is also an interesting clue in the same Rigvedic hymn.

The hymn describes the gods bringing forth Sūrya, who had been hidden in the ocean.

So it is understandable why later tradition connected Mārtāṇḍa with the Sun.

But there is a problem.

The hymn itself never simply says: “Mārtāṇḍa is Sūrya.”

That's why the story remains debated.

Another Possibility: Mārtāṇḍa and Mortality

The final verse gives us another clue.

Mārtāṇḍa is brought back for birth and death.

Because of this, some interpretations understand him not merely as a solar deity but as a figure connected with mortal existence itself — potentially even with the origin of mortal beings.

This creates two very different ways of looking at him.

One interpretation:

Mārtāṇḍa → Sun → the cycle of rising and setting → birth and death.

Another:

Mārtāṇḍa → mortality → the beginning of the mortal order.

Neither interpretation completely resolves the riddle.

And that uncertainty is precisely what makes the story fascinating.

The “Eight Sons” Are Not the Gods You Might Expect

Another interesting detail is that the number of Ādityas changes across Vedic and later traditions.

The Rigvedic hymn speaks of eight sons of Aditi.

Later traditions develop different lists, and the classical Puranic tradition commonly speaks of twelve Ādityas.

So the familiar idea that there has always been one fixed list of twelve Ādityas should not simply be projected backward onto this particular Rigvedic passage.

The mythology was developing.

The identities and relationships of these divine figures changed across different periods and texts.

Mārtāṇḍa sits right inside that fascinating transition.

So Was He Really a Forgotten Sun God?

Maybe.

But saying simply:

“Mārtāṇḍa was the ancient name of Surya.”

misses the strangest part of the story.

The oldest text gives us something much more mysterious:

Aditi gives birth to eight.

Seven are taken to the gods.

One is rejected.

That one is Mārtāṇḍa.

Then he is brought back.

And his existence becomes connected with birth and death.

Later traditions increasingly connect his name with the Sun.

But the original hymn leaves us with an unanswered question:

Why was the Sun — if Mārtāṇḍa was indeed the Sun — the one child Aditi cast away?

The Sun That Dies Every Evening?

There is a fascinating symbolic possibility here.

The Sun appears every morning.

It disappears every evening.

It is born.

It dies.

And then it returns.

If Mārtāṇḍa is understood as a solar figure, his connection with birth and death suddenly becomes remarkably appropriate.

The Sun is the celestial body that seems to repeat this cycle every day.

But we should be careful here.

This is an interpretive reading, not something the Rigveda explicitly explains for us.

The hymn gives us the mystery.

Later traditions and interpreters give us different ways of understanding it.

What Happened to Mārtāṇḍa?

Perhaps the strangest answer is:

He wasn't exactly forgotten.

His name survived.

His story survived.

His association with the Sun survived.

But the mysterious figure who appears in Rigveda 10.72 became increasingly absorbed into later understandings of Sūrya and the Ādityas.

The strange eighth son gradually became less strange.

And perhaps that's why we rarely hear his original story today.

We remember Surya.

We remember the Ādityas.

But we don't often stop and ask:

Who was the eighth son?

The Forgotten Eighth Son

Mārtāṇḍa is one of those figures who makes ancient mythology feel less like a collection of neatly finished stories and more like something that evolved over time.

The oldest text doesn't hand us a complete biography.

It gives us fragments.

An ancient mother.

Eight sons.

Seven accepted.

One rejected.

A strange name.

A return.

And the mystery of birth and death.

Perhaps Mārtāṇḍa was an early solar figure.

Perhaps he represented mortality.

Perhaps the ancient hymn deliberately allowed both ideas to exist.

We don't know with certainty.

And maybe that's the most interesting thing about him.

Because sometimes the oldest stories don't answer our questions.

They leave them behind.

Beyond the Obvious

Mārtāṇḍa is not simply a forgotten name for the Sun.

He is a glimpse into a much older layer of Indian religious thought — a time when the identities of gods were not always as fixed and familiar as they became in later tradition.

And somewhere inside that ancient hymn is an unanswered question:

Why would Aditi cast away one of her own sons — only to bring him back for birth and death?

We may never know exactly what the ancient poet intended.

But the question has survived for thousands of years.

And perhaps that is why Mārtāṇḍa deserves to be remembered.

Sources & Notes:

The story of Mārtāṇḍa appears in Rigveda 10.72.7–9. Translations differ, particularly in how Mārtāṇḍa's name and role are interpreted. Later traditions associate Mārtāṇḍa with the Sun, while interpretations of the original Rigvedic figure remain debated.
